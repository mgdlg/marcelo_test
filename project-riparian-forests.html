Riparian Forests & Urban Bird Diversity

Full English integration of the research project on resident terrestrial bird diversity in the urban successional environment of the Santa Catarina River (Monterrey, Mexico).

  • Organization: Lab of Conservation Biology & Sustainable Development — UANL
  • Role: Researcher & GIS analyst
  • Year: 2024

Project Overview

This page translates and integrates the complete content of the research report titled “Diversity of resident terrestrial birds in the urban successional environment of the Santa Catarina River for different land use and vegetation types.” The study combines point-count bird surveys with spatial analysis of riparian vegetation to quantify diversity patterns and inform conservation and urban planning decisions.

Introduction

The Santa Catarina River (RSC) is a meandering lotic system in the Río Bravo–San Juan basin in Nuevo León, northeastern Mexico. It originates in the La Purísima canyon in the Sierra de San José and flows into the San Juan River over roughly 160 km. Within the Monterrey Metropolitan Area (MMA), it crosses the municipalities of Santiago, Santa Catarina, San Pedro, Monterrey, Guadalupe, Juárez, and Cadereyta.

The RSC channel has long been targeted for large-scale projects. Urban growth throughout the 20th century encouraged widespread infrastructure in and around the riverbed, compromising hydrologic safety and biodiversity. Between the latter half of the century and 2010, river channelization aimed to generate public revenue through entertainment and informal commerce. However, major storms (Gilbert 1988, Emily 2005, Alex 2010) highlighted the risk of treating the riverbed as a fixed urban space, because the ephemeral channel becomes a destructive force during intense rainfall, threatening floodplains in central Monterrey and Guadalupe.

Rapid urbanization has reduced green space per capita and widened the gap between growth and planning. Public concerns about privatization of urban green spaces (such as Fundidora Park) and the hydraulic limitations of the river channel emphasize the need for ecological integrity over urban occupation. Organized citizen collectives now advocate for environmental protection, water security, and healthy ecosystems.

The “One Health” paradigm is gaining traction in Monterrey, recognizing the interconnectedness of human and environmental health. For instance, protests in 2024 opposed transforming the Libertad Park into a hospital, prompting relocation of the proposed facility and reinforcing the importance of urban green space.

Urbanization is a leading driver of biodiversity loss. Semi-arid cities can still retain native biodiversity in marginal habitats, and restoration efforts can convert urban green spaces into biodiversity hotspots. Birds are powerful indicators for urban ecology, with diverse functional guilds and well-documented habitat selection.

Background

Urban river restoration is associated with reduced pollution, higher biodiversity, and improved ecosystem services. In riparian corridors, bird diversity increases with landscape complexity, protected areas, forest cover, and heterogeneous urban matrices. The Santa Catarina River connects key protected areas such as Cerro de las Mitras State Natural Area, La Huasteca Ecological Park, and Cumbres de Monterrey National Park.

A major challenge is the dominance of exotic plant species, which correlates with reduced bird diversity. The RSC is in a secondary successional stage, arrested by introduced species at all strata (e.g., Leucaena leucocephala, Ricinus communis, Cyperus involucratus, Melinis repens). Researchers advocate reforestation with native trees and grasses; native tree species include black willow (Salix nigra) and Mexican poplar (Populus mexicana). The understory is dominated by African grasses that displace native vegetation. Grass cover offers critical foraging areas for granivorous birds.

Human activity further reduces bird richness and abundance, especially in areas with high pedestrian traffic, which is crucial when evaluating the proposal to turn the RSC into a linear urban park. Riparian ecosystems can still support high bird diversity despite fragmentation, suggesting conservation importance even in disturbed contexts.

Objectives

  • Estimate bird richness and biodiversity across vegetation structure and land-cover categories.
  • Evaluate differences between communities using Hill numbers and the Chao–Sorensen similarity index.

Hypothesis

Native riparian forest patches increase bird diversity and serve as ecologically important sites. Lower richness is expected in exotic vegetation and treeless sites, with low similarity between communities and more exclusive species in wooded riparian areas.

Methodology

Study Area and Sampling

The study polygon is centered at 25°40′8.9436″ N, 100°17′39.0984″ W in a highly disturbed urban segment of the RSC. The area spans 1.27 km² and extends about 8.02 km from east to west. Forty randomized point counts (40 m radius) were conducted in early April 2024, immediately after winter migration. Each count lasted five minutes, with a three-minute settling period. Observers recorded species by sight and vocalization. Thirty-nine points were sampled because one location was inaccessible.

Spatial Analysis and Vegetation Variables

Spatial classifications were provided by Sociedad Sostenible, A.C. (SOSAC) and derived from drone orthomosaic telemetry. Eighteen land-use and vegetation classes were consolidated into three categories: riparian forest (RF), exotic vegetation (EV), and no apparent vegetation (NAV). Riparian forest includes native woody genera (Salix, Populus, and some Platanus) plus native secondary, riparian, and subshrub communities. Exotic vegetation includes introduced African grasslands, reedbeds, and extensive stands of castor bean, palms, and guajes. The NAV category covers exposed clay/gravel riverbed and pavement remnants.

For each point, the internal area and percentage of each category were calculated. Points were assigned to the dominant category: 22 in exotic vegetation, 9 in no apparent vegetation, and 8 in riparian forest.

Detectability was lower in dense vegetation (RF and EV) than in exposed sites (NAV). To avoid bias, NAV points were excluded from comparative analyses. A parallel analysis compared sites with at least one tree (wooded) vs. sites without trees, measuring the area of riparian forest sensu stricto (willows, poplars, sycamores). Twenty-six points had trees and thirteen did not.

Diversity Analysis

Diversity was estimated using Hill numbers: observed species richness (q0), Shannon exponential (q1), and inverse Simpson (q2). Chao 1 was used for richness estimation, emphasizing rare species. Sample completeness was computed as observed richness divided by estimated richness. EstimateS 9.10 ran 1,000 bootstrap randomizations for 95% CI. PAST 4.0 provided analytical confidence intervals for diversity indices. Community similarity used the Chao– Sorensen estimator. Aquatic species were excluded to reduce observer bias from conspicuous waterbirds.

Results: Global Diversity and Vegetation Types

The global observed richness was 32 species (95% CI: 24.57–37.43). Extrapolated richness to a common sampling effort (N = 22) was 24.87 species for riparian forest and 22 for exotic vegetation.

Chao 1 estimated global richness at 40.96 species (95% CI: 33.39–72.55). Estimated richness by vegetation type was 17.94 species for riparian forest and 62.2 species for exotic vegetation, reflecting the low sampling effort in some categories.

Sample completeness for the overall survey was 75.7% (95% CI: 42.7–92.8). Completeness was 78.0% for riparian forest and 35.4% for exotic vegetation.

Results: Tree Cover vs. No Trees

For wooded vs. treeless sites, observed richness extrapolated to N = 26 samples was 26 species (95% CI: 18.86–33.14) for wooded sites and 24.55 species (15.72–33.38) for treeless sites.

Chao 1 estimated richness at N = 13 samples was 29.81 species (95% CI: 20.5–82.21) for wooded sites and 23.93 (19.06–51.29) for treeless sites. Completeness was 66.0% and 75.2% respectively.

Discussion

Biodiversity surveys rarely detect all species in a community, particularly when rare species are abundant. This study achieved higher representativeness when comparing wooded vs. treeless sites than when comparing riparian forest and exotic vegetation. Exotic vegetation showed lower recorded richness, likely due to its dominance over large stretches of the riverbed. Riparian forest patches were few, small, and isolated, inflating completeness metrics. Globally, sampling was limited; additional effort is needed for a full inventory of terrestrial birds.

Vegetation structure appears more important than vegetation type alone. Tree cover yielded clearer differences in richness and diversity than the riparian vs. exotic comparison. Studies that integrate both composition and structure typically perform better at explaining avifaunal diversity.

A total of 31 terrestrial bird species and 9 aquatic species were recorded (20% of Nuevo León’s resident bird species). Among 25 families, Columbidae, Icteridae, and Tyrannidae were most abundant. Tyrannidae had the highest richness (five species). Notable species include the Mexican parrot (Psittacara holochlorus; NOM-059-SEMARNAT-2010 “Threatened”) and two regional near-endemics: olive sparrow (Arremonops rufivirgatus) and the northern broad-billed hummingbird (Cynanthus latirostris).

Observed richness was lower than other regional riparian studies, likely due to sampling effort. For example, the Río La Silla study documented 152 species over two years, and iNaturalist records list 117 species for the RSC. Logistical constraints (up to six observers per point) influenced detectability and abundance estimates.

Riparian forests positively influence bird diversity, even in urbanized landscapes. Wooded sites in the RSC showed higher richness and diversity than treeless sites. Species frequently recorded in wooded sites included Great Kiskadee (Pitangus sulfuratus), Golden-fronted Woodpecker (Melanerpes aurifrons), and Lesser Goldfinch (Spinus psaltria). Introduced house sparrow (Passer domesticus) also concentrated in riparian forest. Conversely, Mexican Finch (Haemorhous mexicanus) was recorded only in treeless sites, using patches of Asteraceae for perching and foraging.

Diversity profiles were broadly consistent between analyses, but PAST4 indicated significant differences across q-orders for wooded vs. treeless categories. This aligns with findings that riparian systems can support diverse bird communities despite fragmentation and disturbance, especially at intermediate levels of urbanization.

Conclusions

Native riparian trees are ecologically important for bird diversity in the RSC. While some analyses showed higher richness in wooded sites, overall sampling was insufficient to fully capture terrestrial bird richness. Future research should expand sampling, integrate vegetation structure and composition at finer scales, apply distance sampling to reduce detectability bias, and include multiple seasons. Conservation and water-security goals require evidence-based planning and a more nuanced approach to managing exotic vegetation.
