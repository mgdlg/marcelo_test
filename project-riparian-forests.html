Riparian Forests & Urban Bird Diversity

Multispectral remote sensing and biodiversity surveys to understand how riparian forests shape urban bird communities.

  • Organization: Lab of Conservation Biology & Sustainable Development — UANL
  • Role: Researcher & GIS analyst
  • Year: 2024

Project Overview

This page summarizes and translates the complete research report titled “Diversity of Resident Terrestrial Avifauna in the Urban Successional Environment of the Santa Catarina River for Different Land Use and Vegetation Types.” The study integrates multispectral remote sensing, land cover mapping, and point-count bird surveys to understand how riparian forests shape urban bird diversity in Monterrey, Nuevo León.

Introduction & Context

The Santa Catarina River (SCR) is a meandering, lotic river in the Río Bravo–San Juan basin in northeastern Mexico. It originates in the La Purísima canyon in the Sierra de San José and runs roughly 160 kilometers to the San Juan River. Within the Monterrey Metropolitan Area (MMA), it passes through Santiago, Santa Catarina, San Pedro, Monterrey, Guadalupe, Juárez, and Cadereyta.

The river corridor has long been targeted for urban development, and the expansion of the MMA during the twentieth century led to intensive infrastructure projects that threaten both regional water security and biodiversity. Major hurricanes such as Gilberto (1988), Emily (2005), and Alex (2010) demonstrated the risks of modifying the riverbed, especially for floodplain neighborhoods in central Monterrey and Guadalupe. These histories have fueled civic opposition to privatization and linear-park proposals that would reduce green space per capita and further constrain the river’s hydraulic capacity.

The study is framed by the “One Health” paradigm, which recognizes that human health and environmental health are inseparable. Urbanization drives biodiversity loss in semi-arid cities, yet riparian corridors can still sustain native species and become biodiversity hotspots when restoration and conservation efforts align. Birds are particularly useful indicators for urban ecology, given their well-documented habitat selection and functional diversity.

Research Objectives & Hypothesis

  • Estimate bird richness and diversity for different vegetation types and structures.
  • Evaluate the magnitude of differences between communities using Hill numbers and the Chao–Sorensen similarity index.

Hypothesis: Native riparian forest patches in the Santa Catarina River increase bird diversity because they provide ecologically important habitats. Lower richness is expected in exotic vegetation and treeless sites, with lower similarity between communities and a higher percentage of exclusive species in tree-dominated riparian forest.

Methods

Study Area and Sampling

The study polygon is centered at 25°40′8.9436″N, 100°17′39.0984″W and covers 1.27 km² across roughly 8.02 km of the river in central Monterrey. Sampling occurred during the first four days of April 2024, near the end of the winter migration season. Forty randomized point counts with a 40 m radius were planned, and 39 were completed (point 35 was inaccessible). Each count lasted five minutes with a three-minute settling period. Four to six observers recorded birds visually and by call.

Vegetation Classification

Spatial data and land-cover classifications were provided by Sociedad Sostenible, A.C. (Adrián Lozano, M.C., and Roberto Alviso). Eighteen land-use and vegetation classes derived from a drone orthomosaic were grouped into three categories: riparian forest (RF), exotic vegetation (EV), and no apparent vegetation (NAV). Riparian forest includes Salix, Populus, and Platanus along with native riparian shrubs (mesquite, gavia, palo verde, and others). Exotic vegetation includes African grasses, reed beds, and extensive stands of castor, palms, and leucaena. No apparent vegetation includes exposed clay/gravel riverbed and remaining paved patches from channelization. Of 39 point counts, 22 were classified as exotic vegetation, 9 as no apparent vegetation, and 8 as riparian forest.

Diversity Analysis

Diversity was estimated with Hill numbers: observed richness (q0), Shannon exponential (q1), and Simpson inverse (q2). Richness was also estimated with Chao1 to emphasize rare species (singletons and doubletons). EstimateS 9.10 produced bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals (1,000 randomizations), while PAST 4.0 provided analytical intervals where bootstrapping was unsuitable. The Chao–Sorensen estimator was used to quantify shared species between communities. Aquatic species were excluded to reduce detectability bias.

Results: Global Diversity and Vegetation Types

Global observed richness was 32 species (95% CI: 24.57–37.43). When standardized to the same sampling effort (N = 22), observed richness was 24.87 species (11.13–38.60) in riparian forest and 22.00 species (14.82–29.18) in exotic vegetation.

Chao1 estimated global richness at 40.96 species (33.39–72.55) for 39 points. By vegetation type, Chao1 estimated 17.94 species (14.55–42.31) for riparian forest and 62.20 species (28.24–281.14) for exotic vegetation.

Sampling completeness (observed richness as a fraction of estimated richness) was 75.7% globally (95% CI: 42.7–92.8%), 78.0% for riparian forest (33.1–96.2%), and 35.4% for exotic vegetation (7.8–77.9%).

Results: Tree Presence vs. Treeless Sites

To evaluate the effect of trees, the dataset was regrouped into sites with at least one tree (n = 26) and sites without trees (n = 13). Observed richness standardized to N = 26 was 26 species (18.86–33.14) for tree-present sites and 24.55 species (15.72–33.38) for treeless sites.

Chao1 richness estimates standardized to N = 13 were 29.81 species (20.5–82.21) for tree-present sites and 23.93 species (19.06–51.29) for treeless sites. Sampling completeness was 66.0% (29.4–90.0%) and 75.2% (35.1–94.4%), respectively.

Discussion Highlights

Sampling completeness was lower for exotic vegetation than for riparian forest, likely because exotic vegetation dominated large stretches of the riverbed, while riparian patches were small and isolated. This spatial structure inflates the completeness index for riparian forest and depresses it for exotic vegetation. The global sample was also limited, underscoring the need for greater effort to fully characterize the terrestrial bird assemblage in the Santa Catarina River.

Differences between riparian forest and exotic vegetation may be better explained by vegetation structure than by vegetation type alone. Studies show that considering both composition and structure yields stronger insights. In this study, bird diversity and richness were generally higher in sites with trees, reinforcing the ecological role of riparian woodland structure in urban river corridors.

A total of 31 terrestrial bird species and 9 aquatic species (families Anatidae, Ardeidae, Charadriidae, Scolopacidae, and Phalacrocoracidae) were recorded, representing about 20% of resident birds in Nuevo León. Columbidae, Icteridae, and Tyrannidae were the most abundant families, and Tyrannidae had the highest species richness (five species). Species of conservation concern included the Mexican parakeet (Psittacara holochlorus), listed as “Threatened” in NOM-059-SEMARNAT-2010, and the Olive Sparrow (Arremonops rufivirgatus) and Broad-billed Hummingbird (Cynanthus latirostris), both regional near-endemics.

Observations showed that tree-present sites were heavily used by species such as the Great Kiskadee (Pitangus sulfuratus), Golden-fronted Woodpecker (Melanerpes aurifrons), and Lesser Goldfinch (Spinus psaltria). The house sparrow (Passer domesticus), an introduced species, was recorded mostly in tree-present sites, especially within riparian forest patches. Conversely, treeless sites supported native terrestrial species such as the Mexican finch (Haemorhous mexicanus), which used extensive Asteraceae patches for perching and foraging.

Conclusions

The study emphasizes the ecological importance of native riparian trees for sustaining bird diversity in the Santa Catarina River corridor. However, the sampling effort was insufficient to fully capture terrestrial bird richness. Future work should expand temporal coverage and incorporate vegetation structure with improved detectability models. The results also suggest caution in indiscriminately removing exotic vegetation, since the evidence for lower diversity in exotic patches remains inconclusive without more robust sampling.
